# Manuscript and response-letter prose

Use this reference for drafting, revising, or reviewing a manuscript or response letter, and for research paragraphs in other documents. It does not require a new paper outline, scientific audit, or experiment.

## Write for the paper's reader

Make each sentence serve the paper's argument. Use connected prose to explain how the evidence supports the claim, with procedural detail where readers need it to reproduce the method. Keep reviewer correspondence and the authors' working process out of manuscript prose. Explain disagreement with a reviewer in the response letter, factually and briefly. Do not add a manuscript paragraph merely to disparage a suggested citation or pre-empt criticism.

Work from the active text. Commented-out source counts as deleted unless the user says otherwise, so do not cite it, derive from it, or describe it as the paper's content.

Describe a method by what it compares, why that comparison answers the paper's question, and what it finds. An input list followed by what a framework does with the inputs reads as an interface description, as in “For a specified target, input states, time horizon, and accuracy, the framework assesses a split and compares accepted circuit candidates.” Keep that shape for a formal problem statement or a reproduction recipe. Replacing “the framework” with “we” while keeping the list does not repair it. A better sentence names the comparison, as in “We compare circuits that reach the same accuracy for given input states and evolution time.” A sentence presenting the paper's framework as its contribution can stay when the following sentences say what the framework compares and finds.

Keep code variable names, file layouts, and irrelevant implementation decisions out of the paper. Translate the program's working vocabulary into the scientific objects it stands for, because readers know the objects rather than the code. A candidate pool is the set of circuits or methods compared, a readout or stored time is an observation time, a call is a pulse or gate, an array column is an input–output pair, a scan or search is the range of settings tried, an archived record is computed data, and an internal label for a construction, such as a “strict sandwich,” is the construction itself. Name a solver or optimizer only where the choice affects the result, and keep “solver” for one kind of numerical method. Terms with a methodological role, such as training and held-out inputs, cutoffs, and tolerances, stay in their defined sense, and the text says which choices each input set informed. State mathematics in the paper's notation, and state the convention the paper uses instead of “typically.” Report units, normalization, solver tolerances, cutoffs, initial conditions, and algorithm choices when they affect interpretation or reproduction, in Methods, captions, supplementary material, or a referenced configuration. An undisclosed code convention must not silently shape a formula or numerical claim. Point to the code repository where readers need it, usually once in the methods and once in the data statement.

Report results as quantities and comparisons, such as an error against its threshold or one gate count against another. Define the acceptance criteria once, in the methods, under one name, and elsewhere say that a circuit meets them or give its margin. A verdict in place of a result reads like a test log, as in “the passing Duffing circuits,” “violates at least one criterion,” “Native failure at these inputs,” or “Both satisfy the numerical criteria.” Fold such a statement into the sentence that reports the quantity, or drop it when a table shows the values. Write a criterion the authors chose as their choice, as in “We accept a circuit if its state error is at most \(\sqrt{10^{-3}}\),” and keep “must” for physical, mathematical, or external requirements.

Describe the actual runs. Budget exhaustion is not convergence to a poor local minimum, and adaptive numerical quadrature is not automatically Gaussian quadrature. Use the source, data, or logs; flag a claim that cannot be verified.

For every revision-driven addition, check whether a reader unfamiliar with the review correspondence or revision plan can understand it. Review prompts include `requested`, `suggested`, `as noted in the response`, `(cited source)`, `declared`, `pre-registered`, `frozen`, and `we report it as such`. Keep a term when the paper itself defines and needs it. Replace working-note shorthand with the underlying scientific fact, use the document's established citation/attribution convention, and delete self-praise about reporting it. Combine this check with the shared final gate.

## Give paragraphs a clear hierarchy

- Add interpretation, consequence, or a necessary bridge after a theorem, table, or equation. Do not merely restate the adjacent result. Repeat a headline finding where an independently read abstract, caption, or conclusion needs it.
- Give a detailed limitation one authoritative explanation, in the methods or a limitations paragraph, with concise local qualifications wherever a claim would otherwise mislead. Keep every necessary condition; avoid repeated defensive paragraphs and caveat stacking.
- Do not bolt qualifier upon qualifier onto an existing sentence. Rework the paragraph only when its argument requires it or the user requests structural revision; follow the local-edit constraints in [copyediting](copyediting.md).
- Use a contrast only when the alternative was just discussed or is a standard assumption the reader needs to distinguish. Repeated unnecessary foils obscure the main point.
- Three or more occurrences drawn from `but`, `while`, `whereas`, `rather than`, `by contrast`, `cannot`, `not`, and `only` trigger a paragraph-level review. This is an audit trigger, not a writing quota; keep logically necessary distinctions.
- Avoid `First, ... Second, ... Third, ...` scaffolds in results prose, symmetric two- or three-part constructions as the default paragraph shape, runs of equal-length sentences, and inventories that give every case equal weight. Lead with the finding that matters and subordinate secondary observations without deleting information.
- A bridge or announcement sentence must state a necessary logical connection. Remove sentences whose only purpose is to announce the next topic, such as “The benchmarks support four design conclusions.” Put a cross-reference inside the sentence whose claim needs it rather than in a sentence of its own.
- State the one or two numbers that carry the conclusion and cite the table for the rest. Row-by-row narration adds no interpretation and obscures the main finding.
- Keep metrics and their reporting order uniform across comparable instances. Do not vary the reporting contract to create stylistic variety.
- Give each quantity class one displayed precision and one representation, decimal or percentage, chosen from its resolution or from the paper's existing tables, and use it in text, tables, captions and the response letter. Values added to an existing table in revision follow that table's column format. An approximate prose range marked with ≈ may use fewer digits. Exact counts and identifiers keep their exact form. A set parameter keeps one written form, such as 0.1 rather than 0.1 and 0.10. Write a distance or error as a decimal when a percentage could be read as another quantity, such as an infidelity.
- When a quantity lies near one and its distance from one carries the result, report the complement. For example, report an infidelity 1 − F = 1.3 × 10⁻⁴ rather than F = 0.99987.
- Round once, from the unrounded result. Compute differences, ratios and other derived values before rounding, and check values that fall on a rounding boundary.
- A stated bound (`at least`, `at most`, a floor or ceiling) must hold for the unrounded value of every instance it covers. Round a lower bound down and an upper bound up.
- Give each summary section its own job. The abstract states the problem, the method, and the headline evidence, including the one or two numbers that carry it, in terms a reader can follow before the paper defines its labels and without a definite reference to something not yet introduced, such as “the two benchmarks.” The introduction establishes the problem, the gap, and the contribution, and a passage on related work ends with how this paper differs. The discussion explains mechanisms and the conditions under which the results hold, and the conclusion states what was found and its limits. Reuse exact terminology and verified numbers, but do not copy one summary sentence into several sections, and add an outlook only when the results and limitations support one. A paper with a summary section or theorem chain gives that section the job of organizing the contribution.
- Make each caption readable on its own. Define its symbols and labels in the caption instead of referring to the text, and do not repeat a body sentence verbatim. Text inside figures, such as panel titles, legends, and axis labels, is part of the paper's prose and uses the same terms. When figure edits are authorized, change the figure's source, regenerate the file, and inspect the rendered page for clipped labels and missing glyphs. Otherwise list the label changes for the author.
- Punctuate a displayed equation as part of its sentence. End it with a period when the following text starts a new sentence, such as one beginning “Here.”

## Compose sentences without changing the claim

Use sentence length to clarify the argument. A short pivot must still be a supported technical statement, not a figurative or conversational substitute. Technical register takes priority over rhythm. Remove a sentence that only restates an adjacent quantitative result instead of finding a decorative paraphrase.

Lead with a methodological reason when it explains the choice, without imposing that order everywhere. Name the authors' agency when reporting a real decision; stock `we note` or `we emphasize` framing adds no agency. Use an integrated or parenthetical table reference according to its function, without forced alternation. Give a qualification its own sentence when that makes clear which claim it limits.

Treat clusters of stock openers, repeated `We + verb` sequences, paper roadmaps, balanced inventories, discourse markers, and decorative hedges as prompts for judgment, and do not repair a run of `We + verb` by handing the verbs to abstract nouns. Terms such as `leverage`, `framework`, `promising`, `powerful`, `paradigm`, `robust`, `significant`, and `comprehensive` are not automatic targets. In a copyedit, remove a phrase for a concrete defect such as generic praise, redundancy, ambiguity, or unsupported scope. A requested style rewrite can change sentence and paragraph organization more broadly. Keep necessary technical meaning and stable terminology. Use [detector evaluation](detector-evaluation.md) for explicitly requested detector comparisons.

## Check a manuscript before delivery

Add these checks to the review stage and fix what fails:

- Each set of criteria, each compared set, and each recurring object has one name, defined once. No word carries a software sense or a second meaning the field reads differently.
- The compared set is stated once in detail. The abstract, captions, and table notes keep the short qualifiers their claims need, and no hedge recurs before every noun.
- The abstract carries the headline numbers, defines or avoids internal labels, and refers back only to things already introduced.
- Each quantity keeps one precision and representation, and each set parameter keeps one written form.
- Captions define their own symbols, figure text uses the paper's terms, and displayed equations are punctuated as sentences.
